The 57 Bus

by Dashka Slater

The 57 Bus is a work of nonfiction by journalist Dashka Slater, published in 2017. It's written for a young adult audience but has been widely read by adults as well, and won several awards, including a Stonewall Book Award.

The book tells the true story of an incident that occurred in Oakland, California, in 2013, involving two teenagers who rode the same city bus line — the 57 — but attended different high schools and moved in very different social worlds.

  • Sasha was a white, agender teen (using they/them pronouns) attending a private school, known for being intellectually curious, into linguistics and math, and part of a close-knit, supportive friend group.
  • Richard was a Black teen from a low-income East Oakland neighborhood, attending a public school, trying to stay out of trouble and build a better future for himself and his family.

One afternoon, as a prank, Richard set fire to the skirt Sasha was wearing while they slept on the bus, causing severe burns. The incident led to Richard being charged as an adult with a hate crime, sparking intense media attention and forcing both communities to grapple with questions of race, class, gender identity, juvenile justice, and the consequences of a split-second decision.

Slater's reporting is notably even-handed — she doesn't flatten either teenager into a simple villain or victim, instead exploring:

  • Gender identity — an accessible explanation of what it means to be agender/nonbinary, and Sasha's journey of understanding their own identity
  • Racial and economic disparity — how Richard's background and the justice system's treatment of Black youth shaped the case's outcome
  • The juvenile justice system — questions about whether trying teenagers as adults, and hate crime enhancements, serve justice or simply punishment
  • Empathy and complexity — the book resists easy narratives, asking readers to hold compassion for both teens simultaneously

The book is structured almost like an extended piece of long-form journalism, moving between Sasha's and Richard's perspectives and weighing in courtroom proceedings, interviews, and social context.

It's frequently used in classrooms and book clubs as a way to open discussions about identity, restorative justice, and unconscious bias, and is generally recommended to readers interested in social-justice-oriented nonfiction or true stories that resist tidy moral conclusions.

The Perks of Being a Wallflower

by Stephen Chbosky

The Perks of Being a Wallflower is a coming-of-age epistolary novel by Stephen Chbosky, published in 1999. It's written as a series of letters from the protagonist to an anonymous, unnamed recipient — addressed only as "Dear friend" — which gives the book an intimate, diary-like quality.

The story follows Charlie, an introverted, sensitive high school freshman in the early 1990s near Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Charlie is still reeling from the recent suicide of his best friend and the earlier death of his beloved aunt Helen, and he struggles to navigate the social world of high school. Over the course of his freshman year, he's taken under the wing of two seniors, stepsiblings Sam and Patrick, who introduce him to their circle of friends, first loves, music, and the world of "participating" in life rather than just observing it.

Through his letters, Charlie writes candidly about:

  • First experiences — first parties, first relationships, first exposure to drugs and alcohol
  • Mental health — the novel deals openly with depression, trauma, and Charlie's own psychological struggles, building toward a revelation about his past late in the book
  • Sexuality and identity — including Patrick's experience as a gay teen in a less accepting era
  • Family dynamics and loss — grief, complicated family relationships, and the search for belonging
  • The pain and beauty of adolescence — the book is often remembered for its bittersweet, aching tone about the fleeting nature of youth

The novel became something of a touchstone for a generation of teen readers, prized for its honest, unfiltered portrayal of subjects that were often sanitized in young adult fiction at the time. Its frank treatment of drug use, sexuality, and abuse has also made it one of the more frequently challenged or banned books in American schools and libraries.

Chbosky wrote and directed the 2012 film adaptation himself, starring Logan Lerman, Emma Watson, and Ezra Miller, which is generally regarded as a faithful and well-received translation of the book.

It's often recommended to readers who enjoy character-driven, emotionally raw coming-of-age stories — though given its content, it tends to resonate most with older teens and adults rather than younger readers.

Becoming

by Michelle Obama

Becoming is the memoir of Michelle Obama, published in November 2018. It became a massive bestseller, selling over 10 million copies in its first year and being translated into more than 30 languages — one of the best-selling memoirs of all time.

The book is structured in three parts, tracing her life chronologically:

  • "Becoming Me" — Her childhood on the South Side of Chicago in a working-class Black family, her education at Princeton and Harvard Law School, and the beginnings of her legal career.
  • "Becoming Us" — Meeting and marrying Barack Obama, her career transition into public service and nonprofit work, balancing motherhood with her professional life, and the grueling experience of his early political campaigns, including the 2008 presidential run.
  • "Becoming More" — Her eight years as First Lady, covering initiatives like Let's Move! and Let Girls Learn, life inside the White House with her daughters, and the challenges of being one of the most scrutinized women in the world — including candid reflections on race, public image, and the pressures placed on her family.

What distinguishes Becoming from a typical political memoir is its intimacy and voice. Obama writes in a warm, conversational style and is notably candid about personal struggles — including a miscarriage, using IVF to conceive her daughters, and going to couples counseling with Barack during a difficult stretch of their marriage. She also reflects frankly on navigating racism and sexism, both before and during her time in the White House.

Thematically, the book centers on identity and self-invention — the idea that "becoming" is an ongoing process rather than a fixed achievement, a message that resonated widely with readers regardless of political affiliation, and helped the book cross into a broader cultural phenomenon (including a Netflix documentary and a companion journal).

It's often recommended to readers interested in memoir, women's leadership, or American political history, though it stays more personal than policy-focused — readers looking for a deep dive into Obama-era politics may find it leans more toward the emotional and biographical.

The Alchemist

by Paulo Coelho

The Alchemist by Paulo Coelho is a short, fable-like novel first published in Portuguese in 1988. It's become one of the best-selling books in history, translated into dozens of languages.

The story follows Santiago, a young Andalusian shepherd who dreams repeatedly of finding treasure at the Egyptian pyramids. After consulting a fortune-teller and a mysterious old king named Melchizedek, he sells his flock and sets out on a journey across North Africa to pursue this "Personal Legend" — Coelho's term for one's true calling or life purpose.

Along the way, Santiago meets a series of guides and obstacles: a con man who robs him, a crystal merchant he works for in Tangier, an Englishman studying alchemy, and, most significantly, Fatima, a woman he falls in love with at an oasis, and an alchemist who becomes his mentor and teaches him to listen to his heart and read the omens of the desert.

The novel is really a philosophical parable dressed as an adventure story. Its central themes include:

  • Following your dreams — the idea that the universe conspires to help those pursuing their true purpose
  • The journey over the destination — treasure, in the end, isn't quite what Santiago expects
  • Listening to intuition — Coelho frames the "heart" and natural signs as sources of wisdom
  • Alchemy as metaphor — transforming base metal into gold mirrors the process of self-realization

Coelho writes in simple, accessible prose, which is part of why the book has resonated with such a broad international audience — though that same simplicity has also drawn criticism from some readers who find its aphorisms ("when you want something, all the universe conspires in helping you to achieve it") a bit too tidy or sentimental.

It's often recommended as a quick, reflective read for anyone at a crossroads or thinking about purpose and risk-taking, though whether its optimism feels inspiring or a little glib tends to depend a lot on the reader.
